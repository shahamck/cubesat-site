A Basic Guide
to Nanosatellites

The CubeSat standard was born in 1999, a joint project between California Polytechnic State University and Stanford University to give student teams one shared size and one shared launch interface.

The original aim was simple: make access to space affordable for university researchers.

Key Facts
about CubeSats

Dipole antenna system

Four tape-spring antennas ride coiled up during launch, then spring out to carry uplink and downlink.

Standard format structure

A 10 cm aluminium frame with hard-anodised rails that slide through the launch dispenser and carry every load on the way up.

Solar panel

Triple-junction cells on each face turn sunlight into power, while sun sensors report which way the craft is facing.

Payload

The reason it flies. Here a compact camera and image processor, swappable for science instruments or radios.

Electrical power system

Harvests, stores and routes power through the battery, regulators and the toroidal inductors that keep every rail stable.

UHF/VHF radio

UHF and VHF radios trade range, power and licensing. Which band is right depends on the mission and its ground stations.

Flight control computer

The on-board brain: it runs attitude control, schedules the payload and keeps the craft safe when something goes wrong.

CubeSats started as student projects in low Earth orbit. A few of them have since gone much further.

The standard

One unit,
many sizes

Everything is measured in units. A 1U is a 10 cm cube; bigger craft stack or tile units so they still fit a standard dispenser.
